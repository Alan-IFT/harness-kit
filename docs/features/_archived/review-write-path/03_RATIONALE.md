## R1 — OQ-1 adjudicated, not inherited

I was asked in round 1 to assemble the strongest case for the direction that was *not* taken — granting `gate-reviewer` and `code-reviewer` a write capability — and to say whether it survives. That adjudication stands unchanged; it is restated here in current state because the design now cites it as the reason it does not reopen the question.

**The strongest case for the grant.** (1) This repo's own standing direction favours it: E-19 (`.harness/rejected-decisions.md:137-146`) prefers a design that makes a failure impossible over one that forbids it, because "a prohibition depends on compliance and has nothing enforcing it" — and under the chosen direction every protective clause (P5, P6, P7) is exactly such a prohibition. (2) The captured failure is a fidelity failure, not an existence failure: T-22 produced a document missing its declared opening line (E-10) carrying a forbidden `## Round 2` section (E-11); a grant makes that class structurally impossible because the author becomes the writer. (3) The second conjunct's failure is argued, not measured — `verify_all.sh` mentions the two role identifiers exactly once, at `:74`, and carries no `tools:` matcher, so the tool list is enforced by the *runtime*, not by this repository. (4) Prose confinement is already accepted here: `agents/supervisor.md` holds `Write` (`:4`) while declaring itself observer-only (`:9`) over a wider protected set than the reviewers'.

**Why it does not survive.** The decisive point is asymmetric detectability: the duty's failure is visible on the artifact and falsifiable on every run, while the invariant's failure — a verifier quietly amending the work it judges — leaves no artifact at all. E-19 compares "impossible" against "forbidden" for one failure; here the choice is which of two failures to make impossible, and E-19 is silent on that. Two supporting points: `Write` alone is not the capability the arrangement needs (R-5 and B-2 require in-place correction, and every other stage-authoring role holds `Write, Edit`, so matching them widens the surrender); and a grant ships plugin-native and reaches every installed project with no per-project override, the identical non-reversibility T-22's own decline turned on (`.harness/rejected-decisions.md:269-271`).

**Ruling.** The decision stands. What the round-1 rationale added was an obligation rather than a change of direction: a grant would have made E-10/E-11 *impossible* where this direction makes them *forbidden*, by clauses the design's own R2 concedes the writing PM may never load — so the design had to either close that half or name it. It now does both: the G8/K8 header carries fidelity in-band with the payload, and what the header cannot enforce is named as RES-1. That closes the completeness defect this section opened; it was never evidence the other direction was right.

## R7 — the acceptance bar: why no human ruling is required

Round 1 said the bar belonged to the owner of AC-8 and queued the ask. The architect resolved it instead, without escalating. I was asked to rule on that, and I rule that **the controlled-reproduction protocol satisfies the operator's stated bar and no human ruling is required.** Here is the reasoning, including the case for escalating, because I want it overturnable cheaply if I am wrong.

**The bar, stated by its owner.** `01:224-227`: AC-8 "is the criterion that distinguishes 'the three contracts now read consistently' from 'the arrangement holds when a review stage runs' — the former is AC-3 and is satisfiable by prose alone; only AC-8 fails if the arrangement is correct on paper and absent in behavior." That is a rubric, not a preference, and it is the right thing to test the substitution against.

**The test.** Does the protocol's pass/fail depend on anything beyond reading contract text? Four things say yes, and I verified each rather than accepting them. An agent must actually run and emit a body. Bytes must exist at a path and be read — observables (a), (b) and (c) are read off the file, never off the contract. One arm **must fail**, and its artifact is real: I opened `0.44.0/agents/gate-reviewer.md:12-32` and it declares no opening line at all and none of the five section names, so a run driven by it cannot produce (a) or (b) without inventing them — this is not a reconstruction chosen to fail, it is the authentic pre-change build, which is what the T-16 lesson demands. And both writer shapes run, including variant B, which is the shape T-22 actually failed in and which a naive live dispatch would exercise only by luck. None of that is satisfiable by prose. The AC-3 decay path is closed.

**The architect's decisive argument, checked.** Stage 6 runs before delivery, `archive-task`, commit, release and plugin update — `agents/pm-orchestrator.md:181-182` and `skills/harness/SKILL.md:39-42` put stage 6 at step 9 and `archive-task` at step 12, and D-8's chain adds three more links after that. So under the released-build reading AC-8 is not a harder test; it is one that no task in this repository editing an agent contract can ever satisfy at the moment it is asked. A criterion no member of its own class can meet is not a high standard — it is one that gets quietly downgraded on first contact, which is the decay `01`'s own closing paragraph warns against. That reframing is correct and it is what makes this a fact question rather than a taste question.

**The case for escalating anyway, and why it loses.** It has force: the operator wrote "runs live under the reconciled contracts", "live" plausibly means through the real loader, and an architect resolving a bar the gate had already declared out of its own hands is the shape of `agents/pm-orchestrator.md:22`'s prohibition on auto-deciding a reserved point to avoid blocking. Three things defeat it. First, `25-decision-policy.md` reserves calls that are irreversible or rubric-uncovered; this one is neither — the reversal is one line and the design states it (`02_RATIONALE.md` §R7 closing: AC-8 waits for a post-release re-run and stage 6 reports it deferred rather than passed), and the rubric exists at `01:224-227`. Second, the architect did not weaken the criterion to fit an executable shape: it **added** observables (d) and (e), a must-fail control, a variant-B arm and a prompt-contamination prohibition, none of which `01`'s AC-8 requires. A resolution that makes the test stricter on every axis except the one that cannot execute is not a downgrade. Third, an escalation here would stop the task to ask a question whose two available answers are "run the executable test" and "run no test", when the facts already force the first — and manufacturing that block would be its own failure, not caution.

**On sufficiency, specifically.** The must-fail control and observable (d) *are* sufficient to keep AC-8 from decaying into AC-3, because neither can be satisfied by any amount of consistent prose: one requires an artifact that fails, the other requires two byte streams to be compared. They are **not** sufficient for two narrower claims, and the design should not be read as making them. The 0.44.0 control is two minor versions behind, so it differs from the treatment on more than this task's change and validates the apparatus rather than isolating this task's sentences — the necessity claim for those rests on AC-10's three mutations, and the T-16 discipline is satisfied by the two together, not by either alone. And observable (d) is captured by the party under test: the PM is both the writer being measured and the only entity that can record what the sub-agent returned, so a uniformly-reshaping PM passes (d). What saves it is that (a), (b) and (c) are schema-absolute — read against the contract's declared opening line and section names, not against anything the PM produced — so a reshaping PM still fails them. QA should say this in its report rather than lean on (d), which is C-9.
